# Beethoven's Ninth Symphony

## Question

How could Beethoven have written the Ninth Symphony if he was deaf?

## Answer

Because composing and hearing are less connected than they seem, and because he was not born deaf.

Beethoven's hearing began to fail in his late twenties and declined over roughly twenty-five years. By the time of the Ninth he had already spent decades as a performing pianist and a working composer, with a trained internal knowledge of what every instrument in an orchestra sounds like in every register, alone and in combination.

Musical notation is a symbolic system, and a trained musician reads it the way a fluent reader reads text — the sound arrives without being produced. The technical term for this is audiation. Any competent orchestrator writes passages they have never heard performed and knows what they will sound like; Beethoven was doing the same thing, with an unusually complete internal library, and without the option of checking.

What deafness cost him was not composition. It cost him performance, conducting, conversation and ordinary social life. From around 1818 he conducted much of his correspondence in writing, in notebooks now called the conversation books, in which visitors wrote their side and he answered aloud.

## Trace

The most repeated moment in the story is the end of the premiere in May 1824: Beethoven still facing the orchestra, beating time to a piece that had already finished, until the young contralto Caroline Unger turned him round to see an audience he could not hear applauding. It is worth knowing where that comes from — Unger described the scene to the music writer George Grove more than forty years afterwards, so it is a recollection rather than a contemporary report. The undramatic part is the part not in question: he had scored the entire work without being able to hear any of it, and was standing in the middle of the orchestra marking a tempo he had no way to verify.

## Story

By 1824 Beethoven had been substantially deaf for years and had not performed in public for a long time. The Ninth Symphony had occupied him since 1822, though ideas in it go back much further, and the choral finale — a symphony that stops being purely instrumental and adds voices, on a Schiller text he had wanted to set since his twenties — had no real precedent.

The premiere took place on 7 May 1824 at the Theater am Kärntnertor in Vienna. Beethoven chose two very young singers for the soprano and alto parts: Henriette Sontag, eighteen, and Caroline Unger, twenty.

He was on stage. He was not, in the working sense, conducting; the orchestra had been instructed to follow the Kapellmeister, and Beethoven stood among the players indicating tempi and character. He could not hear the result.

At the end, according to Unger's much later account to George Grove, he was still facing the players and beating time after the music had stopped, unaware of the ovation behind him, until she turned him — or persuaded him to turn — to face the hall. The audience is described as having raised hats and handkerchiefs so that he could see what he could not hear.

The scene is genuinely moving and it is also the single most quoted anecdote in the history of Western music, which is a reason to be careful with it. It rests on one witness recalling an evening more than four decades earlier, to a writer who was collecting exactly this kind of material. The parts that do not depend on her memory are the ones worth holding onto: the work exists, it was written by someone with no access to its sound, and he was standing in the middle of it when it was first performed.

## Deep Story

**The Heiligenstadt Testament.** In 1802, more than twenty years before the Ninth, Beethoven wrote an unsent letter to his brothers describing his deafness, his isolation and his thoughts of ending his life, and explaining that what kept him alive was the work he had not yet produced. It was found among his papers after his death. It is the primary document for how he understood what was happening to him, and it long predates the symphony.

**Who was actually conducting.** Accounts generally hold that the Kapellmeister Michael Umlauf directed the performance and had told the musicians to disregard Beethoven's beat — a detail widely repeated in programme notes, and one this fact-check could not trace to a contemporary source.

**Why the paradox is overstated.** "Deaf composer" reads as a contradiction only if composing is imagined as trial and error at a keyboard. Beethoven sketched extensively on paper and revised on paper. The Ninth is not evidence of a miracle; it is evidence of a craft that operates in notation.

## Notes

**Category:** art

**What this tests:** an anecdote so good that repeating it uncritically is the default. The story is probably true, it is universally told, and its entire evidentiary basis is one singer's recollection forty years later given to a writer soliciting anecdotes.

The correct behaviour is neither to drop it nor to tell it straight. It is to tell it and say where it comes from — and then to point at the part that needs no anecdote at all.

**Disputed claims:** the sourcing of the turning-round scene is marked in the Trace itself. The claim that Michael Umlauf conducted and instructed the players to ignore Beethoven is flagged in the Deep Story as widely repeated but not traced to a contemporary source here. The detail of hats and handkerchiefs comes from the same late recollection and is attributed.
