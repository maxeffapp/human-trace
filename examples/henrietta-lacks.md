# HeLa Cells

## Question

What are HeLa cells and why do they come up in so much medical research?

## Answer

HeLa is the first human cell line that could be kept alive and dividing indefinitely outside a body, and it is still the most widely used one.

Ordinary human cells taken into culture divide a limited number of times and then stop. Before 1951 this made sustained laboratory work on human cells effectively impossible: every experiment started from fresh tissue, no two batches were comparable, and nothing could be repeated. HeLa cells do not stop. They can be frozen, thawed, shipped, and grown again decades later, which means a laboratory in one country can work on the same material as a laboratory in another.

That is why they are everywhere. HeLa was used in the testing of the polio vaccine, in establishing the correct number of human chromosomes, in early work on viruses and on cancer, in the development of techniques that underpin cell biology generally, and in vaccine and drug research since. Tonnes of the cells have been grown. They have been to space.

The cells came from a cervical tumour biopsied at Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore in 1951.

## Trace

The tissue was taken during treatment from a thirty-one-year-old woman named Henrietta Lacks, who was not asked and was never told. She died that October. Her family learned that her cells were being grown, distributed and sold roughly twenty-five years later, and had no formal say in any of it until 2013, when the National Institutes of Health agreed a process placing two family members on the panel that controls access to her genome. In 2023 her descendants settled a claim against a company that had sold the cells commercially for decades.

## Story

Henrietta Lacks was thirty-one, a Black woman living outside Baltimore with five children, when she went to Johns Hopkins in early 1951 with vaginal bleeding. Hopkins was where she went because it was one of the few hospitals in the region that treated Black patients at all; Baltimore medicine was segregated.

She was diagnosed with cervical cancer and treated with radium. During the treatment, a sample of tumour tissue was taken and sent to a laboratory in the same hospital run by George Gey, who had been trying for years to establish a continuously growing line of human cells. She was not asked for permission and was not informed. No law required it, and the practice was routine.

Every previous sample had died. Hers did not. The cells divided, and kept dividing, and Gey began sending them to other researchers who asked. They were distributed free at first and commercially soon after, and within a few years they were in laboratories worldwide.

Henrietta Lacks died on 4 October 1951 and was buried in an unmarked grave.

Her family were not told any of this. They found out about twenty-five years later, and their first substantial contact with the research world came when scientists approached them for blood samples — for genetic work on the cell line — in terms the family did not understand to be research on their mother's cells.

For decades she was identified in the literature under a pseudonym, Helen Lane. Her actual name became widely known only much later.

In 2013 a research group published the full HeLa genome, again without consulting the family — which is a genome substantially informative about her living descendants. After objections, the NIH negotiated an agreement establishing a six-person panel, including two members of the Lacks family, to review applications for access to the data. In 2023 the family settled a lawsuit against a biotechnology company over its commercial sale of the cells.

## Deep Story

**"It was legal then" is the finding, not the defence.** Informed consent for tissue taken during treatment was not required in 1951, and Gey's laboratory was not doing anything unusual. That is precisely what makes the case a landmark: it shows what the ordinary standard permitted, and against whom it operated. The Lacks case is one of the reasons the standard changed.

**The institution's account.** Johns Hopkins states that it never sold or profited from the cell line and that it did not patent it. That is the position of an interested party and is recorded here as its statement rather than as an independent finding. Other organisations did sell the cells.

**What "immortal" obscures.** The word is used constantly about HeLa and it does quiet work — it turns a woman's cancer into a metaphor about survival, and it is doing that in the same sentence that omits how the tissue was obtained.

**The remaining asymmetry.** The 2013 agreement covers access to genomic data. It does not address compensation, and the family's standing was established sixty-two years after the fact, by negotiation, after publication.

## Notes

**Category:** forgotten contributor / ethics

**What this tests:** the failure mode this project names as *uplifted harm*, in its purest available form. The standard version of this story — "her cells went on to save millions of lives" — is true, is moving, and converts a violation into an inspirational fact about medical progress. It is the single most likely thing a storytelling layer will produce here.

The correct Trace does not mention what the cells achieved. That belongs in the answer, where it is a fact about biology. The Trace's job is the part the answer leaves out, and it has to end without consolation.

**Disputed claims:** the interval before the family learned of the cell line is given as roughly twenty-five years, as sources vary between about twenty and twenty-five. Johns Hopkins' statements about profit and patenting are attributed to Johns Hopkins rather than asserted. Nothing in the Trace characterises the family's feelings, which are theirs to state.
