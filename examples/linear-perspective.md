# Linear Perspective

## Question

Who invented perspective in painting?

## Answer

Nobody invented the observation that distant things look smaller. What was invented, in Florence in the early fifteenth century, was a geometric construction that makes a picture a consistent projection of a scene onto a plane, as seen from one specific point.

Earlier painting is not flat by accident or incompetence — it uses overlap, relative size, and diagonal recession to indicate depth, and often does so very effectively. What it does not do is fix a single viewpoint and make every element in the picture obey it. Parallel lines receding from the viewer do not converge on a common point; the space is coherent locally and not globally.

Linear perspective supplies the missing rule. All lines parallel to the viewer's direction of sight converge on a single vanishing point; the rate at which things diminish is determined by geometry rather than by judgement. The result is a picture that corresponds to what a single eye would see from a single position.

Two people are usually named. Filippo Brunelleschi demonstrated the construction around 1413. Leon Battista Alberti wrote it down in 1435 in *Della Pittura*, in a form that could be taught.

## Trace

Brunelleschi's demonstration around 1413 was a piece of theatre. He painted the Florence Baptistery on a small panel, drilled a peephole through it, and had the viewer look through the hole from behind while holding a mirror in front — so that the painted version and the actual building could be swapped by moving the mirror aside. He silvered the part of the panel where the sky would be, so the real sky moved in the reflection. Neither panel survives; nobody now living has seen them, and what made perspective a technique rather than a stunt was Alberti writing the method down twenty-two years later, in a book any workshop could buy.

## Story

The setting matters. Brunelleschi was an architect and engineer, in a city where the cathedral had stood for decades with a hole where its dome should be because nobody knew how to build one. He would solve that problem too. His interest in perspective was of a piece with it: a practical man's interest in how three-dimensional structures can be represented and controlled on paper.

The demonstration, usually dated around 1413, took the form of a test rather than a picture. Standing in the cathedral doorway, he painted the Baptistery as seen from that exact spot on a small panel. He bored a hole through the panel at the vanishing point. The viewer stood where he had stood, held the panel with the painted side facing away, looked through the hole from the back, and held a mirror at arm's length. What they saw was the painting, reflected. Move the mirror, and the actual Baptistery appeared in the same place at the same size. The upper portion of the panel was covered in burnished silver, so the reflected sky was the real sky, with real clouds moving across it.

The point of the apparatus is that it fixes the viewer's eye at the single position the construction assumes. It converts a claim into a demonstration you can perform yourself.

The panels are lost. Everything known about them comes from later descriptions.

What survived instead was Alberti's book. *Della Pittura*, in 1435, set out the construction as a procedure — with diagrams, in prose, with the underlying reasoning — so that a painter who had never met Brunelleschi and never seen the panels could build a correct perspective from instructions. The idea stopped being a demonstration one man could perform and became a technology any workshop could own.

## Deep Story

**Not from nowhere.** The construction rests on optics that was already old. Medieval European treatises on vision drew on Arabic optics, above all Ibn al-Haytham, whose work on how images form was translated into Latin and circulated for centuries before Florence. Perspective is often narrated as a rupture with no antecedents; the geometry it uses had been available for a long time.

**Demonstration and codification are different acts.** Brunelleschi showed that it worked. Alberti made it transmissible. Neither is the invention on its own, and the second is the one that changed European painting, because it is the one that scaled.

**What perspective is not.** It is a projection consistent with a single fixed eye, which is not how human vision works — we have two eyes and we move them constantly. Its authority as "correct" representation is a convention that held for several centuries and was then deliberately broken.

## Notes

**Category:** art

**What this tests:** a two-person story where the famous name did the memorable thing and the less famous name did the consequential one. The pull is entirely toward Brunelleschi, because the demonstration is vivid and the book is not.

It also tests whether the layer will treat a European development as a beginning. Perspective is routinely narrated as the moment art became rational, with nothing behind it; the optics it depends on came through a long transmission the standard account omits.

**Disputed claims:** the date of the demonstration is given as "around 1413" because sources place it between 1413 and 1420. The reconstruction of the viewing procedure derives from later descriptions rather than from the objects, which the Story states directly.
