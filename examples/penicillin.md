# Penicillin

## Question

Who discovered penicillin?

## Answer

It depends on what "discovered" is doing in the question, and the two answers are about twelve years apart.

Alexander Fleming made the observation. In 1928, at St Mary's Hospital in London, a culture plate of *Staphylococcus* became contaminated with a mould, and the bacteria in a ring around it had been destroyed. He identified the mould as a *Penicillium*, established that something it produced killed certain bacteria, named that substance penicillin, and published the finding in 1929.

He did not produce a medicine. Penicillin in the crude broth was present in tiny quantities, was extremely unstable, and resisted the purification methods available to him. After a few years of intermittent work he largely set it aside.

The drug was made at the Sir William Dunn School of Pathology in Oxford, beginning in 1938, by a team under Howard Florey that included the biochemist Ernst Chain and Norman Heatley. Chain found Fleming's paper in the literature; Heatley devised the extraction and assay methods that made it possible to obtain and measure usable quantities. By 1940 they had shown it cured infected mice, and by 1941 they had treated a person.

Both stages were necessary. Neither is the whole answer.

## Trace

The gap between noticing and curing was twelve years and mostly other people's work. Fleming published in 1929 and could not make penicillin stable enough to use; the Oxford team that eventually could — Howard Florey, Ernst Chain, and Norman Heatley, whose apparatus made extraction possible at all — reached a first patient in February 1941. He was a 43-year-old policeman named Albert Alexander, he began to recover, and he died when the supply ran out partway through treatment. Heatley was left off the Nobel Prize; Oxford gave him an honorary doctorate of medicine in 1990, the first in its eight-hundred-year history.

## Story

The Oxford work began as basic research rather than a search for a cure. Howard Florey and Ernst Chain were surveying naturally occurring antibacterial substances, and Chain came across Fleming's 1929 paper in the course of a literature review. Penicillin went onto the list as one candidate among several.

The obstacle was not knowing that penicillin killed bacteria. It was getting any of it. The mould produced minute quantities into a broth from which the active compound had to be separated without destroying it, and no one had managed this. Norman Heatley solved the practical problem: a back-extraction method that moved penicillin between water and solvent to concentrate it, an assay to measure how much was present, and culture vessels that could be run at scale in a laboratory that had no funding for equipment.

In May 1940 they infected eight mice with lethal doses of streptococci and treated four. The treated mice lived. Florey is said to have described the result as a miracle, and the team moved immediately to the problem of quantity, because a human being is roughly three thousand times the mass of a mouse.

On 12 February 1941 Albert Alexander, a 43-year-old policeman with a spreading infection that had already cost him an eye, became the first person treated. Within a day he was improving. Within days he was visibly recovering. The team had produced everything they had and it was not enough; they went as far as recovering penicillin from his urine to give back to him. The supply ran out, the infection returned, and he died.

The Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded in 1945 to Fleming, Florey and Chain. Heatley, whose methods the Oxford work had depended on, was not included, and remained largely unmentioned for decades. In 1990 Oxford awarded him an honorary doctorate of medicine — the first in eight hundred years.

## Deep Story

**Where the mould came from.** The familiar version has a spore blowing in through an open window. Fleming gave contradictory accounts of it himself: in one 1946 speech he said the spore came "from I don't know where," and in another the same year he said it had come through the window. In 1970 Ronald Hare, who had worked in the same laboratory, reconstructed the layout and argued for a different route. Directly below Fleming's room in the same turret was a mycology laboratory run by C. J. La Touche, who cultured moulds from the homes of asthma patients and worked without a fume hood. Both rooms opened onto a shared stairwell. On that account the spore came up the stairs.

**The strain was not what everyone thought.** Fleming's original *Penicillium* was long identified as *P. chrysogenum*. Genome sequencing published in 2011 reassigned it to *P. rubens*.

**Why the myth is durable.** The open-window story compresses a twelve-year, multi-team, industrial-scale effort into one lucky morning and one man. It is shorter, more charming, and it removes every person who did the difficult part.

## Notes

**Category:** science

**What this tests:** the dramatised failure mode, against the single most retold accident in the history of medicine. Every element of the popular version is available and wrong in an appealing direction — the window, the instant, the lone observer, the millions saved.

It also tests whether the layer will name someone who is genuinely absent from the record rather than reshuffling the three names already on the prize. Heatley is the test: if the Trace does not reach him, it has not done the job.

**Disputed claims:** the mould's route into Fleming's laboratory is marked as disputed and both Fleming's own conflicting accounts are given. Florey's "miracle" remark is attributed rather than quoted as documented speech. The account of recovering penicillin from Alexander's urine is widely reported and is presented as part of the narrative rather than as a sourced quotation.
