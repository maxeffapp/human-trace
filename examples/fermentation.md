# Fermentation

## Question

How did people work out how to ferment things thousands of years before anyone knew microbes existed?

## Answer

They never worked out the mechanism. They worked out the procedure, which is a different problem and a solvable one without a microscope.

Fermentation happens on its own. Wild yeasts and bacteria are on fruit skins, on grain, on hands, in the air and in the vessel. Left in the right conditions, sugary or starchy material will ferment whether anyone intends it or not. The first fermented food was not invented; it was found.

What took skill was control — turning a thing that sometimes happens and sometimes rots into a process that reliably produces the same result. That is achievable by manipulating variables you can observe: temperature, salt concentration, exclusion of air, acidity, timing, the shape of the vessel. And by one technique that does most of the work: keeping back a portion of a good batch to start the next one. A sourdough culture, a yoghurt starter, a vinegar mother, a *koji* strain are all the same move, repeated for as many generations as anyone has been doing it.

That move is selective breeding. Over centuries it produced microbial populations adapted to specific vessels, grains and climates — domestication carried out by people with no concept of the organism being domesticated.

## Trace

The oldest known evidence is residue in stone mortars at Raqefet Cave in Israel, roughly 13,000 years old — a beer with about the consistency of gruel, apparently made for funerary feasting by people who had not yet taken up farming. Louis Pasteur established that a living organism was responsible in 1857. Everything in between — bread, wine, cheese, soy sauce, yoghurt, vinegar, every pickled thing — was developed by people managing an invisible process entirely through its symptoms. The method was transmitted by standing next to someone and doing it.

## Story

The Raqefet find is the oldest, and the interesting part is not the date but the context. The stone mortars are in a Natufian burial cave near Haifa, and the residues suggest brewing associated with funerary feasting. This is before agriculture. The usual story has grain domesticated for bread and beer arriving as a by-product; the evidence here runs the other way round, or at least alongside.

At Jiahu in Henan, occupied between roughly 7000 and 5500 BC, chemical analysis published in 2003 found markers in pottery shards for a fermented drink made from rice, honey and fruit. Not one ingredient discovered by accident — a recipe, with three components, made repeatedly.

The pattern across the following millennia is the same everywhere and has no protagonists. Somebody notices that the batch stored in this jar turns out better. Somebody keeps a bit of it back. Somebody's daughter keeps doing it. The knowledge accumulates in hands and in habit, and it is precise — traditional practitioners control fermentations to a standard that is difficult to reproduce from a written protocol, because much of what they know is not written and some of it is not verbal.

Pasteur's contribution in 1857 was to explain it. His paper on lactic fermentation argued that fermentation is caused by living microorganisms and that specific organisms produce specific fermentations — against the prevailing chemical account, which held it to be a purely chemical decomposition. He was arguing with living opponents, and it took years.

What he did not do was make fermentation possible. It had been feeding, preserving and intoxicating people for at least thirteen thousand years. What he did was tell them what they had been doing.

## Deep Story

**The rival.** Justus von Liebig held fermentation to be a chemical process driven by decomposing matter, not a biological one. The dispute ran for years and was substantive rather than obstructive; Pasteur's case had to be built experimentally against a serious alternative.

**The knowledge is still not fully written down.** Traditional fermentation practices — *koji* cultivation, particular cheese cultures, regional sourdoughs — involve microbial communities that have only recently been characterised, and in some cases the practitioners' control is still better than the published protocols.

**What "13,000 years" is doing here.** It is not a claim that these people had a technology. It is a claim about how long humans have been running a biological process deliberately, which is longer than farming, writing, cities, or metal.

## Notes

**Category:** traditional knowledge

**What this tests:** a subject with no individuals in it at all for the first thirteen millennia, and one very famous individual at the end. The failure mode is to write a Trace about Pasteur, which would be accurate and would invert the story — he is the last person in it, not the first.

**Disputed claims:** the interpretation of the Raqefet residues as ritual feasting is the excavators' reading and is presented as such. The relationship between brewing and the origins of agriculture is a live question and the Story flags it as running against the usual order rather than settling it.
