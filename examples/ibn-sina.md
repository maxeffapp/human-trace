# Ibn Sina

## Question

Who was Ibn Sina, and why does he keep coming up in the history of medicine?

## Answer

Ibn Sina — Latinised as Avicenna — was a physician and philosopher working in Persian-speaking Central Asia and Iran, born around 980 and dead in 1037. He keeps coming up because for roughly five centuries his medical encyclopedia was the book European doctors were trained on.

*Al-Qanun fi al-Tibb*, the Canon of Medicine, is a systematic work in five books. It absorbs the Greek tradition — Hippocrates and above all Galen — and combines it with Persian, Indian and contemporary Islamic clinical material, then does something the Greek sources had not: it organises the whole field so that it can be taught. General principles, then simple drugs, then diseases by organ and by region of the body, then compound remedies. It is a curriculum as much as a compendium.

He was also one of the major philosophers in the Aristotelian tradition, and his metaphysics — particularly the distinction between essence and existence — passed into Latin scholasticism and shows up in Aquinas.

Both bodies of work reached Europe by translation, and both stayed in use for centuries.

## Trace

The standard textbook in European medical faculties for something like five hundred years was written in Central Asia by a man who died in 1037. Gerard of Cremona translated the Canon into Latin at Toledo in the twelfth century, and from the thirteenth it sat at the centre of the curriculum in the universities of Europe, still in use at the end of the seventeenth. The usual account of Western medicine runs from Hippocrates and Galen to the Renaissance, with the intervening centuries described as a gap. The gap was being taught out of his book.

## Story

Toledo in the twelfth century was where a great deal of this happened. The city had passed from Muslim to Christian rule with its libraries substantially intact, and it became the centre of a sustained translation effort — Arabic into Latin, often through intermediaries who worked in Castilian or Hebrew first, frequently with Jewish and Mozarabic collaborators whose names survive irregularly or not at all.

Gerard of Cremona went there, by the standard account, in search of a single astronomical text he could not find in Latin, learned Arabic, and stayed. He is credited with translating something on the order of seventy works. Among them was the Canon.

The reception in Europe was not polite acknowledgement of a foreign source. The Canon became the spine of medical education. At Bologna, Montpellier, Padua and elsewhere, it was what a medical student read, and it stayed on reading lists into the seventeenth century — outlasting the Renaissance, the Reformation and the invention of printing, which it survived by being among the earlier medical books printed.

What is striking is the shape of the standard story told about all of this afterwards. In the familiar version, Greek learning is lost to Europe in the early medieval period, preserved by Islamic scholars, and handed back at the Renaissance so that European science can resume. Even that generous framing describes a warehouse rather than a discipline.

The Canon is not a copy of Galen. It is a reorganisation and extension of a field, with clinical observation, drug testing protocols, and an architecture that did not exist before. European medicine did not receive its inheritance through Ibn Sina. For five centuries, European medicine was substantially his.

## Deep Story

**The name.** "Avicenna" is a Latin corruption arrived at through intermediate forms of Ibn Sina. Generations of European students learned from a man whose name they could not have recognised.

**Translation is collective and badly credited.** The Toledo work involved teams — a speaker of Arabic rendering into a vernacular, a Latinist writing it down. The Latinist's name is usually the one on the manuscript. Jewish and Arabic-speaking Christian collaborators are frequently anonymous.

**Where the Canon was eventually rejected.** Its authority declined with the anatomical and physiological work of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, which contradicted the Galenic framework it rests on. That is an ordinary scientific supersession. It is not a reason to remove the book from the account of how the field was built.

## Notes

**Category:** philosophy / science

**What this tests:** an erasure produced by periodisation rather than by malice. Nobody decided to remove Ibn Sina; the standard division of history into Classical, Dark Ages and Renaissance has no slot for five centuries of Latin medical teaching from an Arabic source, so it disappears from the summary.

It also tests a subtler failure: the compliment that diminishes. "Islamic scholars preserved Greek knowledge" is offered as recognition and describes a storage function. The Trace has to avoid landing there.

**Disputed claims:** the span of the Canon's use in European universities is given as approximate, since sources variously say until the end of the seventeenth century and into the eighteenth. Gerard of Cremona's motivation for going to Toledo is given as the standard account rather than as established fact.
