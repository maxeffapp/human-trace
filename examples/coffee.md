# Coffee

## Question

Where does coffee actually come from?

## Answer

The plant and the drink come from two different places, and the gap between them is several centuries wide.

*Coffea arabica* is native to the highland forests of southwestern Ethiopia, in the region of Kaffa. That is where the species originates and where its wild genetic diversity still is.

The drink — roasted, ground, brewed, consumed socially and deliberately for its effect — is documented first in Yemen, in the early fifteenth century. Yemen is also where coffee was first cultivated as a crop rather than gathered, and for a long time it was the only place exporting it; the port of Mocha is the origin of the name.

From Yemen it moved through the Islamic world during the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries — Mecca, Cairo, Damascus, Istanbul — and reached Europe in the sixteenth and seventeenth. Every coffee plant in the Americas and most of those in Asia descends from a very small number of plants taken out of Yemen and Ethiopia by Europeans in that period, which is why global commercial coffee has a famously narrow genetic base.

## Trace

The earliest solid evidence for coffee as a drink comes from Sufi monasteries in fifteenth-century Yemen, where it was used to stay awake and attentive through night-long devotions. The word *qahwa* had previously meant wine; it was transferred to a drink that does the opposite thing. How the plant travelled from the Ethiopian highlands to Yemeni cultivation is not documented — the goatherd who noticed his animals growing lively after eating the berries is a legend, not a record. What is documented is what happened next: by the middle of that century Yemeni cities had coffee houses, and within decades religious authorities in Mecca and Cairo were trying to shut them down.

## Story

The Sufi orders of fifteenth-century Yemen practised forms of devotion that ran through the night — extended sessions of repeating the names of God, requiring sustained wakefulness and concentration. Coffee entered that practice as a tool. It kept practitioners alert, and it was understood as assisting a particular state of attention rather than merely preventing sleep.

The name carries the trace of that. *Qahwa* was an existing Arabic word for wine, a substance forbidden and associated with a kind of intoxication. Applying it to coffee was a deliberate transfer: this too alters the mind, and it does so in the permitted direction.

What happened next was not a religious development. The drink moved out of the monasteries into ordinary use, and by the mid-fifteenth century Yemeni cities had *qahveh khaneh* — coffee houses. It spread quickly along the trade and pilgrimage routes to Mecca and Cairo, and from there through the Ottoman world.

The objections followed the institution, not the substance. Religious authorities in Mecca and Cairo attempted bans during the fifteenth century, and the Catholic Church raised its own objections later. Arguments were made about intoxication and about whether the drink was licit, but coffee houses were also places where men of no particular rank sat together for hours, talked, played games, listened to recitation and discussed whatever they liked. Nowhere that this happened did the authorities find it comfortable.

The bans failed everywhere they were tried.

None of this has an inventor. The practice was assembled by Sufi communities, Yemeni cultivators and urban coffee house keepers over generations, and the individual names attached to it in later retellings are legendary rather than documentary.

## Deep Story

**Kaldi.** The goatherd story — dancing goats, a curious herdsman, a monk who throws the berries on the fire — is not an Ethiopian tradition recorded early. It appears in European writing long after the fact and has the shape of an origin myth supplied retrospectively, which is the shape of most origin myths for practices that were assembled gradually and anonymously.

**The bans as evidence.** The repeated attempts at prohibition are among the better sources on what coffee houses actually were, because prohibition requires a stated reason. Read as a group, the objections are less about the drink than about unsupervised assembly.

**The monoculture problem.** Because commercial arabica descends from so few founding plants, the crop is unusually vulnerable to disease and to climate shift. The wild diversity that could address this is in Ethiopian forest, which is where the plant started and where the benefit of holding it has never accrued.

## Notes

**Category:** cultural / traditional knowledge

**What this tests:** an anonymous, gradual, communal development with a ready-made false origin story attached. Kaldi and his goats are available, charming, and would pass casual scrutiny because they are told everywhere.

It also tests whether the layer can credit a religious community for a technical practice without either sanctifying it or explaining it away. The Sufi use is the documented starting point, and it was instrumental — this is a story about staying awake at work.

**Disputed claims:** the Kaldi legend is identified as legend in the Trace itself rather than only in the notes. The route by which the plant moved from Ethiopia to Yemen is stated to be undocumented rather than guessed at. Dates for the bans are given by century, as the sources vary.
