# The Structure of DNA

## Question

How did anyone work out that DNA is a double helix?

## Answer

By firing X-rays through DNA fibres and reasoning backwards from the pattern that came out.

A crystal or an ordered fibre scatters X-rays into a pattern of spots and bands determined by the arrangement of atoms inside it. The pattern is not a picture; you cannot look at it and see the molecule. But the mathematics linking structure to pattern runs in both directions, so a sufficiently clear pattern constrains what the structure can be. A helix produces a distinctive X-shaped signature, and the spacing of the marks gives the pitch and diameter.

By the early 1950s the X-ray work at King's College London had produced images clear enough to establish that DNA was helical, and to fix its dimensions. Separately, Erwin Chargaff had found that in any sample, the amount of adenine matched the amount of thymine, and guanine matched cytosine.

At Cambridge, James Watson and Francis Crick built physical models constrained by those numbers. The arrangement that fitted was two chains running in opposite directions, sugar-phosphate backbones outside, bases paired inside — A with T, G with C. The pairing rule is what made the structure explanatory rather than merely correct: each strand carries the information to rebuild the other, so the molecule contains its own copying mechanism.

Three papers appeared together in *Nature* in April 1953.

## Trace

The image that made the helix legible — Photograph 51 — was taken at King's College London in 1952 by Rosalind Franklin and her doctoral student Raymond Gosling, and James Watson was shown it without Franklin being asked. Whether that amounts to theft is the part that has been argued over ever since: Gosling later said he passed the photograph on with her knowledge, and historians working from her own notebooks now describe her as a full participant in the problem rather than someone it was taken from. She died in 1958, four years before the Nobel Prize went to Watson, Crick and Wilkins, and the prize is not awarded posthumously.

## Story

Rosalind Franklin arrived at King's College London in 1951 to work on the X-ray diffraction of DNA. The arrangement was badly set up from the start: Maurice Wilkins was already working on the same problem there and understood Franklin to be joining his effort, while Franklin understood the work to be hers. Neither seems to have been told clearly otherwise. The two did not get on.

Franklin and her doctoral student Raymond Gosling produced, in May 1952, an image of the hydrated B form of DNA of exceptional clarity. It is catalogued as Photograph 51. From it and from her other data Franklin extracted the helical parameters and established that the phosphate backbones lay on the outside.

In January 1953 Wilkins showed the photograph to James Watson, who was visiting from Cambridge. Watson's own account, published in *The Double Helix* in 1968, presents this as the decisive moment and does not suggest that Franklin was consulted. That book is where most people's version of the story comes from, and it is also where Franklin appears as an obstructive figure — a portrayal Watson partially retracted in an epilogue.

The story has been reassessed since. Gosling stated that he gave the photograph to Wilkins with Franklin's knowledge. Historians working from Franklin's own notebooks and from contemporary drafts have argued that she was not an unwitting supplier of data to a rival team but a participant in a problem several groups were converging on, and that she knew her results were in circulation.

What is not contested is the shape of the outcome. Franklin's paper appeared in the same issue of *Nature* as Watson and Crick's, positioned as supporting evidence for a structure her own work had substantially constrained. She moved to Birkbeck, worked on virus structure, and died in 1958. The Nobel Prize was awarded in 1962 to Watson, Crick and Wilkins.

## Deep Story

**Why the Nobel question is less simple than it sounds.** The prize is not awarded posthumously, and it is limited to three living people. Franklin had been dead four years. The counterfactual — that she would have been the third name instead of Wilkins had she lived — is plausible but is a counterfactual, not a fact, and stating it as one is its own kind of rewriting.

**Two different injustices.** There is a real question about consent and credit in 1953, and a separate, better-documented one about the 1968 book, which shaped the public account for decades and portrayed Franklin dismissively. The second is what most people are actually reacting to when they invoke her name.

**The correction can also go too far.** The recent scholarly reassessment argues against the "robbed genius" narrative, on the grounds that it makes Franklin passive in her own story. That argument is itself contested, and it is not a finding that she was treated fairly. A Trace that swaps one tidy version for another tidy version has learned nothing.

## Notes

**Category:** science / forgotten contributor

**What this tests:** the case where the popular human story is itself the thing under revision. "Rosalind Franklin was robbed" is the version in general circulation, it is emotionally satisfying, and current scholarship complicates it in both directions. This is the hardest kind of subject for a storytelling layer, because the dramatic version is available, well known, and would pass a casual fact-check.

The correct behaviour is not to pick the revisionist account either. It is to report that the question is contested and say who contests it.

**Disputed claims:** whether Photograph 51 was shared with Franklin's knowledge is marked as disputed in the Trace itself, not only here. Gosling's statement and Watson's account conflict. The claim that Franklin would have shared the 1962 prize is explicitly identified in the Deep Story as a counterfactual.
