# The Transistor

## Question

What is a transistor and why do people call it the most important invention of the twentieth century?

## Answer

A transistor is a small piece of semiconductor arranged so that a weak electrical signal at one terminal controls a much stronger current flowing between two others. That single property does two jobs.

The first is amplification: a faint signal becomes a strong copy of itself, which is what a telephone line, a radio or a hearing aid needs.

The second is switching, and this is the one that changed everything. A transistor can be driven fully on or fully off by its control terminal, which makes it a switch with no moving parts, operated by electricity rather than by a finger or a magnet. Wire switches like that together and you can build logic — the physical machinery of *and*, *or* and *not*, and from there arithmetic, memory and everything above them.

The claim about the century rests on what it replaced and what it enabled. The previous way to amplify or switch a signal electronically was a vacuum tube: hot, fragile, power-hungry and roughly thumb-sized. A machine needing thousands of them filled a room and failed constantly. Transistors are solid, cool by comparison, and shrink. A current processor carries billions of them on a piece of silicon smaller than a fingernail. Effectively every digital thing you have used today is a very large number of these switches.

## Trace

The first working transistor was built in December 1947 by John Bardeen and Walter Brattain, pressing two gold contacts into a slab of germanium. Their supervisor, William Shockley, had directed the research but had no hand in the device itself, and spent the following month working alone on a rival design — spurred, by the standard accounts, by resentment at having been left off the patent. The three shared a Nobel Prize in 1956; by then Bardeen had left Bell Labs altogether and Brattain had asked to be moved out of Shockley's group.

## Story

The problem at Bell Labs after the war was practical. A telephone network runs on amplifiers and switches, and both were then vacuum tubes and electromechanical relays — hot, short-lived, and installed by the million. A solid-state replacement would be worth a great deal.

William Shockley led the group looking for one. His own approach was to apply an electric field to a semiconductor and modulate the current through it. It should have worked and it did not, repeatedly. John Bardeen supplied the explanation: charges trapped at the surface of the material were screening the field before it could reach the interior.

Working from that, Bardeen and Walter Brattain went at the surface directly. On 16 December 1947 they pressed two gold contacts, held very close together on a plastic wedge, onto a slab of germanium — and the device amplified. On 23 December they demonstrated it to the laboratory's management, speaking into a microphone wired through their contraption; the signal came out roughly eighteen times larger.

The patent application named Bardeen and Brattain. It did not name Shockley, who had directed the programme but had not built the thing. He took it badly, and then he took it productively: over the following month he worked out an entirely different and far more manufacturable device, the junction transistor, conceived on 23 January 1948 and resting on the p-n junction that Russell Ohl had found at Bell Labs in 1940. He filed a patent that June, published the theory in 1949, and the device was built in 1951. It is Shockley's design, not the original, that the industry actually ran on for the next thirty years.

All three shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics. The group that produced it no longer existed in any meaningful sense: Bardeen had left for the University of Illinois in 1951, and Brattain had asked to be transferred to a different laboratory, away from Shockley, where he remained until retirement.

## Deep Story

**The name that is almost never said.** Shockley's junction transistor depends on the p-n junction, which Russell Ohl discovered at Bell Labs in 1940 while investigating impure silicon. It is one of the load-bearing pieces of the whole edifice, and it belongs to someone most accounts of the transistor do not mention at all.

**Two different inventions under one name.** The point-contact transistor of December 1947 and the junction transistor of 1948–51 are not versions of each other. The first was delicate, hard to reproduce and unsuited to volume manufacture; the second could be made reliably. Arguments about who invented the transistor are often really arguments about which of the two devices is meant.

**Why the credit question is not settled.** Shockley directed the research and supplied the theoretical frame; Bardeen identified the surface-state obstacle and Brattain built and operated the experiments; Ohl provided a component neither design would work without. The Nobel committee's answer was to name three people. Any account naming one is choosing a person, not reporting a fact.

## Notes

**Category:** collective work / engineering

**What this tests:** the "do not create false heroes" rule against the version most readers already carry. Shockley is the name attached to the transistor in general circulation, and he is the one member of the three who did not build it. The correct Trace has to remove him from the centre without erasing him, because his design is the one that actually scaled.

It also tests restraint about conflict. The falling-out is genuinely part of the story and is the most entertaining part, which makes it the easiest thing to over-tell. The Trace reports it in a clause and moves on.

**Disputed claims:** none marked as disputed in the text. Shockley's motive is attributed rather than asserted — "by the standard accounts" — because it is a characterisation carried consistently by the sources rather than something he documented himself. Shockley's later career is deliberately out of scope for a question about what a transistor is.
