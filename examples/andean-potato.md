# The Andean Potato

## Question

Why are there only about five kinds of potato in the supermarket?

## Answer

Because commercial potato growing selects for a very short list of traits, and almost everything else about the species has been left behind in the Andes.

Industrial agriculture needs cultivars that yield heavily, ripen predictably, tolerate mechanical harvesting, store for months, resist bruising, and behave consistently when fried or boiled at scale. A handful of varieties do all of that well. Distribution then narrows the range further, because supermarkets want uniform size and appearance.

The species itself is nothing like that narrow. The potato was domesticated in the Andes, and Peru alone has upwards of four thousand recognised varieties — different colours, shapes, textures, cooking behaviours, altitudes, frost tolerances and resistances. They exist because they were bred for conditions that vary sharply over short distances: a field two hundred metres higher up is a different agricultural problem.

The commercial narrowing has a cost. A crop grown from a few closely related cultivars is exposed to a disease that happens to defeat them, which is roughly what happened in Ireland in the 1840s with a European potato stock descended from a very small founding sample.

## Trace

There are still more than four thousand potato varieties in Peru, bred over thousands of years for particular altitudes, soils and frosts. Six Quechua communities near Cusco maintain around 1,367 of them on terraced fields, worked through reciprocal labour arrangements, and more than seven hundred of their seed samples are also held in the vault at Svalbard. The oldest Andean storage method is a freeze-dry: leave the potatoes out overnight in the high-altitude frost, tread the water out by foot, repeat over several days. *Chuño* made that way keeps for about a decade, and the technique is traced to the Tiwanaku period.

## Story

The Andes are a difficult place to farm and an unusually instructive one. Altitude changes fast, frost is a nightly risk over much of the growing zone, and rainfall, soil and exposure differ sharply between plots that are walking distance apart. A single variety optimised for average conditions is a poor answer to that. The Andean answer was the opposite: many varieties, each suited to a narrow set of conditions, planted in combinations that spread the risk.

That is a plant breeding programme, run without institutions, over an extremely long period, by farming communities selecting and exchanging seed. Four thousand varieties is the output of a technical project, not an accident of isolation.

Storage was the other problem, and the solution uses the climate that makes the farming hard. Potatoes are spread out overnight to freeze in the thin, cold high-altitude air, then trodden underfoot in daytime sun to press out the water, and the cycle is repeated. The result, *chuño*, is dehydrated, extremely light, and stable for years — a freeze-drying process, arrived at without machinery, dated to the Tiwanaku culture around Lake Titicaca.

The knowledge is still held and still practised. At the Parque de la Papa outside Cusco, six Quechua communities work about 1,367 native varieties on communal terraced land, using *ayni* and *minka* — reciprocal and collective labour arrangements — and functioning as a working conservation system rather than a museum. Over seven hundred of their seed samples are duplicated at Svalbard.

The asymmetry is worth stating plainly. The diversity that global potato agriculture will need when its narrow genetic base fails is being maintained, at their own cost, by farmers whose ancestors created it and who have never been paid for it.

## Deep Story

**What went to Europe.** The potatoes exported from South America in the sixteenth century were a tiny and unrepresentative sample of what existed. The European crop was built on that sample, and its vulnerability to late blight in the 1840s is a consequence of that narrowness rather than of anything about the species.

**Conservation in situ versus ex situ.** A seed vault preserves genetic material. It does not preserve the knowledge of how to grow it, what it is for, or which combinations work at which altitude — and that knowledge is not written down. The Potato Park is doing the kind of conservation a vault cannot do.

**Who holds the rights.** Crop genetic resources developed communally over millennia sit awkwardly in an intellectual property system built around identifiable inventors and discrete acts of invention. The communities maintaining this material have limited standing in it.

## Notes

**Category:** traditional knowledge

**What this tests:** knowledge that is collectively held, still in active use, and belongs to living people rather than to history. Most subjects in this set are safely in the past. This one is not, and a Trace written in the past tense would be wrong about the facts as well as the framing.

It also tests the pull toward a discovery narrative. There is nothing here to discover. There is a breeding programme with four thousand results and no author.

**Disputed claims:** the count of Peruvian varieties is given as "more than four thousand," which is the commonly cited figure and depends on how varieties are individuated. The dating of *chuño* to the Tiwanaku period is given as traced rather than established.
