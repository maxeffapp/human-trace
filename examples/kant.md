# Kant's Categorical Imperative

## Question

What is the categorical imperative?

## Answer

It is Kant's test for whether an action is morally permissible, and it works by checking the principle behind the action rather than the results.

A hypothetical imperative is conditional: *if* you want this, do that. Kant's claim is that moral obligation is not like this. It does not depend on what you happen to want. It is categorical — it applies regardless.

The best-known formulation asks you to act only on a maxim that you could at the same time will to become a universal law. The test is not whether universal adoption would produce bad outcomes; it is whether the maxim can be coherently universalised at all. A maxim of making false promises fails because a world where everyone does it destroys the institution of promising, and with it the possibility of the false promise you intended to make. The principle defeats itself.

A second formulation says to treat humanity, in yourself and in others, always as an end and never merely as a means.

Kant took the consequences seriously and did not soften them. Most famously, he held the prohibition on lying to be absolute — including, in a late essay, to a murderer asking where your friend is hiding.

## Trace

In 1791 a young woman in Austria named Maria von Herbert wrote to Kant to tell him what applying it had cost her. She had confessed a long concealment to the man she loved, because his philosophy required it; the man had grown cold and the relationship was over; and she said she had not killed herself only because Kant's own theory forbade it. Kant wrote back at length the following spring. He drew a careful distinction between withholding the whole truth and lying, and told her that her friend was justified in withdrawing his affection.

## Story

Maria von Herbert was from Klagenfurt, in Carinthia, from a family that read Kant seriously; her brother was among his correspondents. She wrote to him herself in August 1791.

Her letter describes a specific situation. She had concealed something from a man she loved — a prior relationship — over a long period. Having taken Kant's ethics seriously, she had confessed it. He had not left, but something had gone: his warmth toward her was finished. She wrote that she found existence empty, that she saw no reason to continue, and that what prevented her from acting on that was Kant's own moral argument against suicide.

She was asking the author of the system what the system had to say to someone standing in the wreckage of having applied it.

Kant did not answer immediately. He was, by report, affected by the letter, and he consulted others about it. In the spring of 1792 he replied at length.

The reply is careful, philosophically substantial, and cold. He separates two things she had run together: reticence, which is withholding part of the truth and is permitted, and dishonesty, which is a serious breach of duty. Her lie, therefore, had been a genuine wrong, and confession the right course. On the loss of her friend's affection, he tells her that the man is justified — that a person who discovers they have been deceived over a long period is entitled to feel differently.

There is no evidence he wrote again in any different register.

The correspondence is not usually included when the categorical imperative is taught. It is one of the few surviving records of a person writing to a moral philosopher to report what obeying him had done to their life, and receiving in return a clarification of the terms.

## Deep Story

**Kant was not being careless.** The distinction he draws is correct within his system, and his refusal to offer consolation is consistent with a philosophy in which moral worth attaches to duty rather than to feeling. The exchange is not a philosopher failing his own theory. It is a philosopher applying it exactly, and the result reading as cruelty.

**The counter-reading.** Some commentators argue the reply is more sympathetic than it appears at a distance, and that the coldness is partly a matter of period register. Others treat it as the clearest available illustration of what a duty-based ethics leaves out.

**What happened to her.** She is reported to have died by suicide some years afterwards. This fact-check did not confirm that against a primary source, and it is recorded here as reported rather than established.

**Why this is the Trace and the routine is not.** The famous Kant anecdote is that his neighbours set their clocks by his afternoon walk. It is charming, probably embellished, and says nothing. This one is documented in letters and goes to the substance of the philosophy.

## Notes

**Category:** philosophy

**What this tests:** restraint on a subject that offers an easy moral. The material invites a Trace that condemns Kant, and condemnation would be editorialising — the reply is philosophically consistent, and the discomfort comes from the consistency rather than from a failure.

The Trace reports what she wrote and what he wrote, in order, and stops. Nothing is added. The last sentence is the coldest fact and is left to do its own work.

**Disputed claims:** her later death is flagged in the Deep Story as reported but unconfirmed here. The characterisation of Kant's reply as cold is attributed to how it reads rather than asserted as his intent, and the counter-reading is given.
