# The Web

## Question

Is the web the same thing as the internet?

## Answer

No. The internet is the network; the web is one thing running on it.

The internet is infrastructure: physical links, routers, addressing, and the protocols that move packets of data between machines that may be on entirely different kinds of network. It does not care what the data is.

The web is an application built on top of that. It consists of three things invented together: a way to name a resource anywhere on the network (the URL), a protocol for asking a server for one (HTTP), and a format for documents that can point at other documents (HTML). A browser is a program that follows those pointers.

The distinction is easiest to see by listing what else uses the internet without being the web. Email predates it. So do file transfer and the domain name system. Video calls, messaging apps, game traffic, software updates and streaming are separate applications with their own protocols, several of which most people use daily without a browser being involved.

The web is the part of the internet made of linked documents. It is enormous and it is not the whole thing.

## Trace

The proposal that became the web was written at CERN in March 1989 and formalised the following year with Robert Cailliau. What made it *the* web, rather than one of several information systems being tried at the time, was a decision taken three years later: on 30 April 1993 CERN released the software on a royalty-free basis, relinquishing its intellectual property rights and permitting anyone to use, copy, modify and redistribute it. A particle physics laboratory gave away what became the most commercially valuable software of the century, on the argument that it would only work if nobody owned it.

## Story

The problem Tim Berners-Lee was trying to solve at CERN was mundane and specific. A large physics laboratory has thousands of people arriving and leaving on short contracts, using incompatible machines, producing documentation that lives on whatever system they brought with them. Information was constantly being lost — not deleted, just stranded on a computer nobody could read any more, or known only to someone who had gone home.

His March 1989 proposal described a way to link documents across incompatible systems so that a reference could be followed without knowing where the thing was or what machine it lived on. It was formalised as a management proposal in November 1990 with Robert Cailliau, a Belgian systems engineer at CERN who pushed it internally.

The technical pieces were assembled quickly: the first browser, the first server, the first pages. The web went outside CERN in 1991.

It was not the only such system, and on the merits it was not obviously the winner. Several networked information systems were in use in the early 1990s, some more capable.

The decision that settled it was legal rather than technical. Berners-Lee and others argued inside CERN that the software had to be free of royalties and restrictions, permanently, or institutions would not adopt it. On 30 April 1993 CERN issued a document putting the code into the public domain — relinquishing, in its own words, all intellectual property rights to it, in both source and binary form, and granting anyone permission to use, duplicate, modify and redistribute it.

CERN exists to study particle physics. It had no commercial interest in the software and no mechanism for exploiting it, and the people involved judged that any licence at all would slow adoption enough to matter. On that reasoning the organisation handed away, without conditions, the software layer on which most of the world's commerce now runs.

## Deep Story

**The document is short.** The 1993 declaration is a page. It is one of the most consequential legal instruments of the century and it reads like an internal memo, because that is what it was.

**Cailliau's part.** Berners-Lee wrote the proposal and the software. Cailliau's contribution was institutional — co-authoring the formal proposal, securing internal support, and arguing the case inside CERN, which is the kind of work that leaves little trace and without which the project is a side project that ends.

**Why "invented the internet" is the wrong phrase for this.** Berners-Lee is regularly described as the inventor of the internet. He invented something that runs on the internet, twenty years after the network itself was built by other people. The confusion is a good illustration of how public credit attaches to the layer users can see.

## Notes

**Category:** collective work

**What this tests:** whether the layer can identify that the pivotal human decision was not the invention. The obvious Trace is about a man having an idea in 1989. The better one is about an institution choosing, in 1993, not to own the result — a decision made by people arguing internally, which is invisible in every popular retelling.

**Disputed claims:** none marked. Claims about why the web prevailed over contemporaneous systems are kept general, because attributing the outcome to any single competitor's licensing decision could not be confirmed against an institutional source during fact-checking. CERN's declaration is paraphrased with a short quoted fragment rather than reproduced.
