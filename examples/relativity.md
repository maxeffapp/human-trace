# General Relativity

## Question

What is general relativity, and how do we actually know it's right?

## Answer

General relativity says that gravity is not a force pulling objects together but a consequence of the shape of spacetime.

Mass and energy distort the geometry of space and time around them. Objects moving through that distorted geometry travel on the straightest available paths, which from outside look like curves. A planet orbits the Sun not because a force is tugging on it but because the Sun has bent the region it is moving through. The theory replaces a force acting instantaneously at a distance with a geometry that responds locally.

We know it is right because it makes predictions that differ measurably from Newton's, and those predictions keep coming out correct.

Mercury's orbit precesses slightly faster than Newtonian gravity allows, by an amount general relativity gets exactly right. Light passing a massive body is deflected, and by twice the amount a Newtonian treatment predicts. Clocks run slower deeper in a gravitational field, which is measurable between the top and bottom of a building and is large enough that satellite navigation systems have to correct for it continuously — without the correction, GPS positions drift by kilometres per day. Massive accelerating objects radiate gravitational waves, detected directly since 2015.

The theory has now been tested across an enormous range of scales, and has not yet failed one.

## Trace

The first test that could have killed the theory needed a total eclipse, and the expeditions sent to catch the one in May 1919 sailed within months of the armistice — British astronomers travelling to Príncipe off West Africa and to Sobral in Brazil to check the work of a physicist in Berlin. The plates gave a deflection of light of about 1.6 arcseconds from Príncipe and about 1.98 from Sobral, which the observers judged to be the amount Einstein's theory demanded. The announcement in London that November is the point at which Einstein stopped being a physicist and became a public figure.

## Story

By 1916 Einstein had a finished theory and one piece of supporting evidence — it accounted for the anomaly in Mercury's orbit that had bothered astronomers for decades. But Mercury was a known problem the theory had been developed alongside. What it needed was a prediction made in advance and then checked.

The candidate was starlight. If mass bends spacetime, light from a distant star passing close to the Sun should arrive along a deflected path, shifting the star's apparent position. The effect is tiny and the Sun is overwhelmingly bright, so the only way to see stars next to it is during a total eclipse.

The eclipse of 29 May 1919 was unusually favourable, and the Astronomer Royal, Frank Dyson, organised two expeditions to observe it. Arthur Eddington went to the island of Príncipe off the west coast of Africa. Charles Davidson and Andrew Crommelin of the Royal Observatory, Greenwich went to Sobral in northeastern Brazil.

Both parties spent months hauling equipment, building shelters and setting up telescopes for a few minutes of totality. At Príncipe it rained on the morning of the eclipse. Eddington got usable plates from a break in the cloud near the end.

The measurements were painstaking and the numbers were not identical: about 1.6 arcseconds of deflection from the Príncipe plates and about 1.98 from Sobral. On 6 November 1919 the results were presented at a joint meeting of the Royal Society and the Royal Astronomical Society in London. The conclusion given was that a deflection of light near the Sun definitely occurs, and that it is of the size Einstein's theory required.

The press coverage that followed was on a scale no scientific result had received before. Einstein, who had spent the war in Berlin, woke up internationally famous — for a theory almost no one reporting on it could follow, verified by astronomers from a country his own had been at war with a year earlier.

## Deep Story

**The accusation of cherry-picking.** The Sobral expedition produced plates from two instruments. One set, from the astrographic telescope, was affected by focus problems and gave a value close to the Newtonian prediction rather than Einstein's. Eddington and Dyson set those plates aside as unreliable. Later critics argued this was motivated reasoning — that a Quaker pacifist keen to see a German theory vindicated after the war had thrown away the data that disagreed with him. Subsequent re-analyses of the original plates have generally supported the decision to exclude them on technical grounds. The episode remains one of the standard case studies in how confirmation is judged, and the argument is not entirely closed.

**The prediction had been wrong once.** Einstein published a value for the deflection in 1911, before the theory was complete, that was half the final figure. An eclipse expedition to test the earlier number was sent to Crimea in 1914 and was interrupted by the outbreak of war, its German members detained. Had it succeeded it would have measured against the wrong prediction.

**What one result can settle.** A single confirmation does not establish a theory. What made 1919 decisive was less the strength of the evidence than that it was a public, advance, falsifiable prediction that came out right. The heavy confirmation came later and continues.
