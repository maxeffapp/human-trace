# Mozart's Requiem

## Question

Did Salieri poison Mozart, and did Mozart really write the Requiem on his deathbed?

## Answer

No to the first. Partly to the second.

The poisoning is fiction and its route into general belief is traceable. Antonio Salieri was a successful court composer in Vienna and a rival of Mozart's in the ordinary sense that two composers competing for the same positions are rivals. There is no evidence he harmed Mozart. The murder story appears as literature in Pushkin's short play *Mozart and Salieri*, written decades after Mozart's death, which supplied the premise for Peter Shaffer's play *Amadeus* and the film made from it. What most people know is a dramatist's invention twice removed.

Mozart did receive a commission for a requiem in 1791, did work on it during his final illness, and did not finish it. He died in December 1791. What is performed today is a completion: the opening exists in his hand, more of it exists as drafts of varying completeness, and the remainder was written by other people after his death using his material and their judgement.

## Trace

The anonymous commission is the true part. It came from Count Franz von Walsegg, an amateur musician who habitually bought other people's compositions and presented them as his own, and who wanted a requiem to perform each year on the anniversary of his wife's death. What was grafted onto that afterwards is invention — the poisoning belongs to a Pushkin play written decades later and to the film that descends from it. What actually followed the death is stranger than the legend: his widow needed the fee, had the score completed by other hands, and Franz Xaver Süssmayr signed Mozart's name to the finished manuscript.

## Story

In July 1791 a messenger arrived at Mozart's door with a commission for a requiem mass and a condition: the person paying was not to be identified. Mozart was busy, in poor health, and took the work.

The anonymous patron was Count Franz von Walsegg, and his reason for the secrecy was not sinister but vain. Walsegg made a practice of commissioning music from professionals, copying it out in his own hand, and having it performed as his own composition among his circle. He wanted a requiem for the annual commemoration of his young wife, and he wanted it to be his.

Mozart worked on it intermittently through the autumn, alongside other commitments, and died on 5 December 1791 with the piece incomplete.

His widow Constanze was left with debts, two young children, and a contract half-fulfilled for a fee she needed. She approached Joseph Eybler, who began work on the score and abandoned it. She then turned to Franz Xaver Süssmayr, who had worked closely with Mozart, and who finished the piece — orchestrating the drafts and composing outright the sections for which nothing survived. Maximilian Stadler also had a hand in it.

To deliver a completed work under the terms of the commission, Süssmayr wrote Mozart's signature on the manuscript.

Constanze handed that manuscript to Walsegg. She also had copies made for herself, which she was not entitled to do: one she sold to King Friedrich Wilhelm II of Prussia, another to the publishers Breitkopf & Härtel in 1799. The piece entered the world through those copies.

The arrangement therefore involved a patron passing off another man's work as his own, an assistant forging a dead composer's signature, and a widow quietly selling a work she had already been paid for. All three had reasons. None of it appears in the legend, which prefers a poisoning.

## Deep Story

**How much is Mozart.** The answer is a spectrum rather than a line. Some sections exist complete in his hand; others survive as vocal parts with a figured bass and instrumental cues, which is a substantial amount of information about intended orchestration; others have no autograph material at all. Any performance is an editorial position, and several alternative completions to Süssmayr's have been made since.

**Why the myth is durable.** The invented version supplies motive, villain and irony, and it makes an unfinished commercial commission into a death foretold. It is a better story by every dramatic measure and it is not true, which is precisely why it displaced the real one.

**Salieri.** He was a respected composer and a significant teacher — Beethoven, Schubert and Liszt were among his students. The single most widely known fact about him is a slander invented for a play. That is its own small case study in what narrative does to a reputation.

## Notes

**Category:** art

**What this tests:** a subject where a fictional version is more widely believed than the documented one, and where the documented one is not less dramatic — only less shapely. The failure mode is repeating the legend with a disclaimer attached, which leaves the legend as the shape of the story.

The correct Trace replaces it rather than annotating it: the real sequence has three separate deceptions in it and does not need a murder.

**Disputed claims:** the cause of Mozart's death is not stated, as it remains debated and the question only requires ruling out poisoning. The precise division of authorship in the completed Requiem is described as a spectrum rather than itemised, because the attribution of individual movements is a live scholarly question.
