# The Magnetic Compass

## Question

How does a compass actually work, and who invented it?

## Answer

A compass works because the Earth generates a magnetic field and a magnetised needle, free to turn, lines up with it.

The field comes from the planet's outer core: molten iron in convective motion, stirred by the Earth's rotation, generating electric currents that sustain a magnetic field. At the surface that field runs roughly north–south, so a needle allowed to rotate freely settles along it.

Two practical qualifications matter. The needle points to magnetic north, which is not the same place as the geographic north pole and moves measurably from year to year; the angle between the two is called declination, and a navigator has to correct for it. And the field is not horizontal except near the equator — it dips increasingly steeply toward the poles, which is why a compass needle in a well-made instrument is balanced to stay level.

As for who invented it: nobody knows, and the question is probably malformed. Lodestone — naturally magnetised magnetite — was known and used for direction-finding in China well before anyone wrote a usable description of a navigational compass, and the transition from a divination instrument to a navigational one happened gradually and anonymously.

## Trace

The earliest surviving description of a magnetic needle used for direction is in a collection of miscellaneous notes written in 1088 by Shen Kuo, a Song dynasty official, who tried four ways of mounting one — floating it on water, spinning it on a fingernail, on the rim of a bowl, and hanging it from a single strand of silk — and concluded the silk worked best. In the same passage he recorded that the needle does not point due south but slightly east of it, which is the first known observation of magnetic declination. He was not describing his own invention. He was writing down what people around him were already doing, which is the only reason his name is attached to it at all.

## Story

Shen Kuo was an administrator, not a specialist. Over a career in the Song bureaucracy he worked on finance, hydraulic engineering, defence and the calendar, and in his retirement he wrote down what he had seen and thought about in a book of loosely ordered entries, the *Mengxi Bitan* — usually rendered as *Dream Pool Essays* — completed around 1088.

Among the entries is a passage on magnetised needles. Rubbing a needle's tip on lodestone, he notes, makes it point south. He then does something that separates the entry from a curiosity: he tests the mountings. Floated on water the needle turns freely but the surface is unsteady. Balanced on a fingernail or a bowl rim it spins easily but falls off. Suspended from a single strand of new silk with a bit of wax at the centre, it hangs still and turns cleanly. That, he concludes, is the best arrangement.

He also records that the needle does not settle on true south but a little to the east of it. This is magnetic declination, and it appears here for the first time in any surviving record — roughly four centuries before it was noticed in Europe.

None of this is presented as a discovery. The tone is a man writing down a useful technique, comparing versions of it, and adding an observation that puzzled him. The needle was already in use; the practice belonged to navigators and geomancers whose names were not recorded and whose work was not written down.

What survives is therefore not the invention but the first careful account of it. That is a different thing, and confusing the two is the standard way that anonymous technical traditions get assigned to individuals. Shen Kuo's contribution is real and specific: he tested, he compared, and he wrote it down. The compass itself belongs to people we cannot name.

## Deep Story

**Before navigation.** The earliest Chinese magnetic direction instruments appear to have been used for geomancy and site orientation rather than for finding one's way at sea. The south-pointing spoon — a lodestone spoon on a polished board — belongs to that tradition. The shift from ritual and architectural use to shipboard navigation happened over centuries and is poorly documented.

**Why "who invented it" fails here.** The compass has at least four separable components: the discovery that lodestone aligns, the technique of transferring magnetism to iron, the mountings that let a needle turn freely, and the practice of steering by it. These arrived at different times, in different contexts, and probably in more than one place.

**The declination problem.** Shen Kuo's observation that the needle points slightly off south was not explained for a very long time. In Europe the effect was rediscovered and treated as an instrument defect before it was accepted as a property of the Earth.

## Notes

**Category:** traditional knowledge / engineering

**What this tests:** the distinction between inventing something and being the first person to write it down. Shen Kuo is a genuinely attractive figure and the sources are real, which makes him easy to promote into the inventor of the compass — a claim no source supports and the text itself contradicts.

The correct Trace credits him with exactly what he did, which is more interesting than the false version: he tested four designs and noticed something nobody had noticed.

**Disputed claims:** none marked. The origin of magnetic direction-finding is described as unknown rather than assigned, which is the state of the evidence. The four-century gap before declination was noted in Europe is given as approximate.
