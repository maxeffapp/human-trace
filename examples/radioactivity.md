# Radioactivity

## Question

What is radioactivity, actually? I hear the word constantly but I've never understood what is physically happening.

## Answer

Radioactivity is what an unstable atomic nucleus does to become more stable: it sheds energy, and usually matter along with it.

A nucleus is a crowd of protons and neutrons held together against the electrical repulsion of the protons. Some combinations of the two are stable indefinitely. Others are not, and a nucleus in an unstable configuration will eventually rearrange itself, emitting something in the process. There are three common forms. **Alpha** decay throws out two protons and two neutrons bound together — a helium nucleus — which is heavy, slow, and stopped by a sheet of paper. **Beta** decay converts a neutron into a proton or the reverse, emitting an electron or a positron, which penetrates further. **Gamma** emission is a high-energy photon released as a nucleus settles into a lower energy state, usually just after one of the other two, and it takes dense material to stop.

The timing is statistical rather than scheduled. No individual atom is counting down; each one has a fixed probability of decaying in any given interval. A half-life is simply the interval over which that probability works out to one half of a large population. This is why a substance's activity is predictable in bulk and completely unpredictable atom by atom.

## Trace

The word itself was coined in a converted shed in Paris, in a paper Marie and Pierre Curie published in 1898. Getting from that word to a tenth of a gram of pure radium took four more years and roughly ten tons of pitchblende ore, dissolved and boiled and crystallised by hand. Neither of them knew what the material was doing to them: Pierre carried a sample in his waistcoat pocket to show friends, and Marie kept a little of it by her bed because she liked the way it glowed in the dark. Her notebooks from those years are still radioactive, and are kept in lead-lined boxes.

## Story

In 1898 Marie Curie took up a puzzle Henri Becquerel had turned over two years earlier: uranium salts fogged a photographic plate in complete darkness, with no obvious source for the energy. Measuring the effect across a range of minerals, she found that pitchblende, a uranium ore, was considerably more active than its uranium content could account for. Something else was in it.

That July she and Pierre published a paper announcing a new element, which they named polonium after the country she had left. The same paper contains the first use of the word *radioactivity*. A second element, radium, followed in December.

Announcing an element and holding it in your hand are different problems. Radium occurs in pitchblende in quantities so small that a visible amount required tons of ore, and the separation work was industrial rather than delicate — dissolving, boiling, filtering, crystallising, then doing it again. They worked in a converted shed in the courtyard of the school where Pierre taught: unheated, poorly ventilated, with a roof that leaked. Marie described stirring vats of boiling material with an iron rod nearly as tall as she was.

It took four years. From roughly ten tons of ore they recovered about a tenth of a gram of pure radium chloride, and Marie determined its atomic weight.

Neither of them understood what they were handling. Pierre liked to carry a sample in his waistcoat pocket to show people. Marie kept a small quantity by her bed for the light it gave off. Both recorded burns, exhaustion and pain without connecting them to the cause, because the connection had not yet been made by anyone.

The 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics nearly went to Becquerel and Pierre alone; the French Academy of Sciences had nominated only the two men. A member of the nominating committee wrote privately to Pierre to warn him, and Pierre replied that a prize for radioactivity that omitted Marie would be a travesty. Her name was added.

She died in 1934 of aplastic anaemia, attributed to cumulative exposure — from the radium years and from the mobile X-ray units she ran near the front during the First World War. Her notebooks from the shed still register on a detector, and are stored in lead-lined boxes. They will be radioactive for roughly another fifteen hundred years.

## Deep Story

**What came before.** Becquerel's 1896 observation was itself accidental in origin — he was investigating phosphorescence and expected sunlight to be involved. The Curies' contribution was to establish that the effect was a property of certain atoms themselves rather than of a chemical or physical state, which is what made a new category of matter necessary rather than a new chemical explanation.

**The second prize.** Marie received the 1911 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the isolation of radium and the determination of its properties — an award for the four years of physical labour in the shed rather than for the original insight.

**The record of the harm.** The Curies' exposure was not exceptional for the period; it was ordinary. Radium was sold as a health product for decades afterward, and the young women who painted luminous watch dials with radium paint — pointing their brushes with their lips — became one of the first documented occupational cohorts to establish the link. That history is a Human Trace of its own and is not Marie Curie's story.
