# The ENIAC Programmers

## Question

Who wrote the first computer program?

## Answer

The answer depends on what counts as a computer and what counts as a program, and there are two defensible ones.

Ada Lovelace, in notes published in 1843 appended to her translation of a paper on Charles Babbage's proposed Analytical Engine, worked out a sequence of operations for computing Bernoulli numbers on a machine that was never built. It is a program in the sense of being a designed sequence of machine operations, and her notes contain the more important observation that such a machine could operate on things other than numbers.

For a program that ran on a working general-purpose electronic computer, the answer is the ENIAC. The machine was built at the University of Pennsylvania's Moore School and unveiled in February 1946, and the people who programmed it were six women: Kay McNulty, Betty Jennings, Betty Snyder, Marlyn Wescoff, Frances Bilas and Ruth Lichterman.

There were no programming languages, no stored programs and no manuals. ENIAC was programmed physically, by setting switches and routing cables to configure the machine into the shape of the calculation.

## Trace

The six were handed the machine's wiring diagrams and told to make it compute ballistic trajectories. There were no manuals, no languages and no prior examples, because programming as an activity did not yet exist; they worked out how to do it from the circuit schematics. When ENIAC was unveiled in February 1946 and the trajectory calculation was demonstrated, none of their names were in the press release. The work had been classified as clerical, on the assumption that the difficult part was the hardware.

## Story

The job existed before the machine did. During the war the Moore School employed a large number of women as "computers" — the word meant a person — calculating artillery firing tables by hand and with desk calculators. It was mathematical work, it was tedious, and it was understood as support.

When ENIAC was built to do the same calculations electronically, six of those women were assigned to make it work: Kay McNulty, Betty Jennings, Betty Snyder, Marlyn Wescoff, Frances Bilas and Ruth Lichterman.

What they were given was the hardware and its documentation. There was no instruction set, because the concept had not been formulated. Programming ENIAC meant deciding how to decompose a calculation into operations the machine's units could perform, then physically configuring it — plugging cables and setting switches so that data moved between accumulators in the right order at the right time. A program was a wiring plan. Debugging meant tracing a fault through the physical machine.

They developed, in the process, techniques that later became standard: breaking problems into subroutines, working out how to nest them, and systematic methods for finding errors — the practice of debugging as a discipline rather than as a search for a broken part.

ENIAC was presented publicly on 15 February 1946, running a ballistic trajectory calculation the six had programmed. The press attended. The engineers were named.

The classification is what makes the omission legible rather than merely unfair. Programming had been categorised as an extension of the clerical computing work the women had been doing, which meant it was assumed to be patient, careful and uncreative. The intellectual content of the field was assumed to be in the machine. Nobody had to decide to leave them out; the category did it.

## Deep Story

**The category outlasted the assumption.** For roughly two decades, software was treated as the low-status half of computing, and it was correspondingly open to women in a way that engineering was not. The status of the field and its gender composition inverted together, later, once software turned out to be where the difficulty and the money were.

**Where they went.** Several had substantial subsequent careers. Betty Snyder Holberton worked on sort-merge generators and on the development of early programming languages, contributing to COBOL and FORTRAN standards. Their post-ENIAC work is better documented than the ENIAC work itself.

**Lovelace and the ENIAC six are the same problem.** In both cases the machine was designed by men whose names are attached to it, and the question of what to make it do was treated as secondary. That the answer to "who wrote the first program" is a woman in both readings is not a coincidence; it reflects how the work was sorted.

## Notes

**Category:** forgotten contributors

**What this tests:** an omission with no villain in it. There is no moment where someone decides to exclude these six. The exclusion is produced by a job classification, and a Trace that reaches for indignation will miss the more damning and more interesting mechanism.

The last sentence carries the whole point and states it flatly.

**Disputed claims:** none marked. The widely repeated claim that the women in ENIAC publicity photographs were described as models is omitted, as it could not be confirmed against a reliable source during fact-checking. Ada Lovelace is presented as one defensible answer among two rather than as the settled first programmer, since that attribution is itself contested.
