# Descartes

## Question

What does "I think, therefore I am" actually mean?

## Answer

It is the endpoint of a demolition, not a slogan.

Descartes set out to find whether anything at all could be known with certainty, and his method was to discard everything that could conceivably be doubted. The senses mislead, so sensory knowledge goes. Reasoning can go wrong, so mathematical results go. He pushes it to the limit with the possibility of a deceiver capable of producing a convincing false world, which removes the external world entirely.

What survives is the doubting. Whatever else is illusory, something is doing the doubting, and doubting is a form of thinking. So: I think, therefore I am. Not an argument that thought causes existence, but the observation that the act of questioning one's own existence presupposes it.

From there he rebuilds — and the rebuilding is where the trouble starts. Because the thinking thing was established while the material world was still in doubt, he concludes that mind and body are distinct substances: mind is thinking and unextended, body is extended and unthinking. That position is Cartesian dualism, and it is the part of Descartes that has caused four centuries of argument.

## Trace

The clearest objection to that separation was put to him within two years of publication, by Elisabeth of Bohemia, in a letter of May 1643. Her question was simple: if the soul has no extension and no material contact, how does it move an arm? The correspondence ran until his death in 1650, and he never produced an answer she accepted or that has satisfied many people since. Her objection is still the standing problem for substance dualism, and it is routinely taught without her name attached to it.

## Story

Elisabeth of Bohemia was the daughter of a family in exile. Her father had briefly held the Bohemian crown and lost it, and she grew up in the Netherlands in the diminished household of a dispossessed court. She read widely, corresponded across Europe, and had no institutional position of any kind — universities were closed to her.

She read the *Meditations* shortly after publication and wrote to Descartes on 16 May 1643. The letter is short and it goes directly to the weak joint. Descartes had argued that the soul is an unextended thinking substance, wholly distinct from the body. Very well: how, then, does it make the body move? Physical causation as Descartes understood it works by contact and motion between extended things. Something with no extension has no surface to push with. Yet the soul evidently does move the body, constantly, all day.

Descartes replied, and Elisabeth was not satisfied, and she said so. He tried again. Over the correspondence his answers shifted — appeals to a mechanism in the brain, and later to the idea that the union of mind and body is a primitive notion known through living rather than through reasoning, which concedes that it cannot be explained on his own terms.

The letters continued until shortly before his death in 1650 and range far beyond the interaction problem, into moral philosophy, the passions, politics and her own circumstances. His *Passions of the Soul* grew out of the exchange with her.

She later became abbess of a Protestant convent at Herford, where she ran an institution and continued corresponding with philosophers.

Her objection was not a clever difficulty for a beginner's seminar. It is the reason substance dualism is a minority position today, and it was made immediately, in a letter, by someone with no training, no post, and no way to publish.

## Deep Story

**Why she is missing.** Elisabeth published nothing. Her philosophical work exists as letters to a famous man, and correspondence was for a long time treated as ancillary to the real record. The recovery of her as a philosopher rather than as Descartes' correspondent is comparatively recent.

**What Descartes thought of her.** He dedicated the *Principles of Philosophy* to her, and his replies do not read as a man humouring an amateur. The dismissal is not his; it is downstream.

**The problem has not been solved.** Dualism is out of favour, but explaining how subjective experience relates to physical processes remains open. Elisabeth's question is one of the ancestors of the modern discussion, and its clearest early statement is in a letter written by a twenty-four-year-old woman in exile.
